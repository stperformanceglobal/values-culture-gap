The Values-Culture Gap

Stated values do not build culture. Deliberately designed processes do. Closing the distance between the two is one of the highest leverage projects available to any leadership group. The evidence for it now spans fifty years.

Simon Thomas  |  ST Performance Global  |  July 2026

01Naming the concept

Walk into most professional organizations and you will find the values within minutes, printed on the wall and woven through the handbook and the onboarding deck. Finding the culture takes longer. It reveals itself over weeks in who gets promoted, in how mistakes are handled and in where leaders choose to spend their time and money. It is not unusual to discover that the culture and the published values are strangers to each other.

The distance between the two has carried a name in the research literature for half a century. Chris Argyris and Donald Schön described the gap between espoused theory and theory in use, which is the difference between what we say guides our actions and what actually does (Argyris and Schön 1974). Edgar Schein later built the same distinction into the most influential model of organizational culture we have, placing espoused values near the surface while locating culture itself in the deeper layer of shared assumptions that actually governs behavior (Schein 2017). At ST Performance we describe the distinction more simply. Values are claims. Culture is evidence.

Values  the claims

Stated principles. The words an organization publishes to define what matters. Usually aspirational and sincerely held. Formed in workshops, codified in policy and displayed with pride. Inert until systems bring them to life.

Culture  the evidence

How people actually behave, especially under pressure and when no one is watching. Formed by what leaders model, measure, reward and tolerate. Lives in daily decisions, promotion patterns and the response to bad news.

The Values-Culture Gap

The measurable distance between the principles an organization publishes and the behaviors it consistently encourages, rewards or tolerates.

02The evidence: fifty years old and freshly confirmed

This is not a rhetorical problem, because it has been measured for as long as most current leaders have been working. The research case here is deliberately built on old foundations because the age of that evidence is part of the argument. These are not modern management fashions responding to a moment. They are findings that have survived five decades of scrutiny and organizational practice, which is precisely what makes them trustworthy. The newer research does not replace them. It confirms with modern methods that the same dynamics are alive in the modern workplace.

The time-tested record

The older record reads like a warning that keeps being reissued. Steven Kerr documented in 1975 that organizations routinely reward one behavior while hoping for another and that the reward system wins every time it conflicts with the aspiration (Kerr 1975). Benjamin Schneider showed in 1987 that organizations attract, select and retain people who fit what the place actually is rather than what it claims to be, which means culture compounds in whichever direction it is already moving (Schneider 1987). Amy Edmondson demonstrated in 1999 that teams learn fastest when members believe they can raise problems without punishment and that this belief is built through repeated leader behavior rather than through announcements (Edmondson 1999). Kotter and Heskett had already shown by 1992 that culture carries real economic weight over a decade or more of performance (Kotter and Heskett 1992).

Fifty years of the same finding

The oldest study cited in this piece predates the personal computer. If the values-culture gap were a management fashion it would have faded decades ago. It has instead been confirmed by every generation of researchers to examine it, most recently with methods and datasets the original authors could never have imagined. Ideas that survive that long stop being theories and start being operating conditions.

The modern confirmation

What the recent research adds is confirmation at a scale the original authors could not have imagined. Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales analyzed the published values of large listed companies and found that advertised values bear no significant relationship to firm performance, while the employee perception that management actually acts with integrity does predict better outcomes (Guiso et al. 2015). Researchers at MIT compared the official values of hundreds of major companies with how employees described daily life inside them and found no correlation between the values a company publishes and how well it lives them (Sull, Turconi and Sull 2020). The same group then analyzed more than a million employee reviews during the resignation wave that followed the pandemic and found that a toxic culture was roughly ten times more powerful than compensation in predicting attrition, which places culture among the most expensive things an organization can get wrong (Sull, Sull and Zweig 2022).

10x

Across more than a million employee reviews analyzed during the Great Resignation, toxic culture predicted attrition roughly ten times more strongly than compensation (Sull, Sull and Zweig 2022). People rarely leave the values on the wall. They leave the culture in the hallway.

The most striking recent evidence comes from inside executive suites themselves. Graham and colleagues surveyed more than thirteen hundred senior executives and found that roughly nine in ten believed improving culture would increase their firm's value while barely one in six believed their own culture was where it needed to be. The study concluded that culture creates value only when day to day practices and norms align with the stated ideals, which is the values-culture gap described in the language of corporate finance (Graham et al. 2022). Advances in method point in the same direction, because when culture is measured from what people actually say and do rather than from official statements it begins to predict real outcomes such as efficiency, risk taking and firm value (Li et al. 2021).

The pattern holds inside elite sport, where the gap tends to show up in staff experience before it shows up anywhere else. A season long study of NBA and NBA G League staff quantified working weeks reaching sixty seven hours with nearly half the competitive season spent away from home, documenting practitioners who absorb player style schedule loads without the recovery infrastructure their organizations build for players (Mercer et al. 2026). Longitudinal work on high performance coaches has likewise linked job insecurity to declining wellbeing across a season (Bentzen et al. 2020). An organization can claim that people come first while its own calendars, contracts and travel patterns say otherwise. In performance environments the calendar is the more honest document.

The cautionary tale remains familiar because it remains instructive. Enron's stated values were respect, integrity, communication and excellence, displayed in the lobby of a company whose operating culture rewarded aggressive dealmaking and punished dissent. None of this evidence means values are worthless. It means values are inert until systems animate them, which makes the gap a design problem rather than a communication problem. Culture pays, but stated values only pay when they become culture.

03Where culture actually forms

If publishing values does not create culture, what does? Schein's answer is precise and slightly uncomfortable for leaders, because culture is embedded primarily through what leaders do rather than through what they announce. His research identifies the primary embedding mechanisms:

  • What leaders pay attention to, measure and control on a regular basis
  • How leaders react to critical incidents and crises
  • How leaders allocate resources, rewards and status
  • Deliberate role modeling, teaching and coaching
  • The criteria used to recruit, promote and exit people

Formal statements of philosophy sit in his secondary category alongside office design and organizational stories, which means the values poster is one of the weakest tools available for shaping behavior (Schein 2017). Kerr's reward folly and Schneider's attraction cycle explain why the gap persists even in well intentioned organizations: the systems keep paying for the old behavior and keep hiring for it, so the culture keeps reproducing itself regardless of what the wall says.

Culture is not the opposite of values. Culture is what values become when they are given a delivery system, or what fills the vacuum when they are not.
Six questions to ask this week

Schein's embedding mechanisms convert directly into diagnostic questions. Honest answers will locate the gap faster than any engagement survey.

  1. Where does each stated value appear in what we currently measure and report?
  2. What did our response to the last crisis teach people about what we really believe?
  3. Where do time, money and status actually flow?
  4. What behavior are leaders modeling when the pressure is on?
  5. What do the last five promotions say we actually reward?
  6. Which of our values could a new hire observe within a week without being told it exists?

04Closing the gap: four moves

The answer is not to abandon values, because written values give a group shared language and a standard to point to when decisions get difficult. The answer is to treat values as design specifications rather than decorations. Four moves make that shift practical.

Move 1Translate values into a critical few behaviors

A value only becomes usable when it is converted into observable behavior, which is why Katzenbach and colleagues argue that lasting culture change comes from focusing on a critical few behaviors: a small set of visible actions practiced by influential people that make the desired culture tangible enough to copy (Katzenbach, Steffen and Kronley 2012). The test is simple and worth applying to every value an organization holds. Could a new staff member watch the group for a week and see the value without being told it exists? If a behavior cannot be observed then it cannot be coached, rewarded or measured.

Move 2Audit the carriers

Take each value and walk it through Schein's embedding mechanisms, asking where it shows up in what gets measured, in how the last crisis was handled, in the budget and in who was promoted last cycle. A value that appears in none of these places is not yet a value but an aspiration waiting for a delivery system. This audit is often the most confronting step and also the most valuable, because it converts a vague sense of cultural drift into a specific list of design work.

Move 3Design processes that carry the value

Culture change fails when it relies on memory and goodwill. It holds when the value is built into the processes people already run, because a process keeps running whether or not anyone is feeling inspired that week:

  • Hiring: interview scorecards that probe for the critical behaviors rather than competence alone
  • Promotion: rubrics that weight how results were achieved as heavily as the results themselves
  • Meetings: agendas and rituals that rehearse the value weekly rather than annually
  • Failure: a review protocol that examines systems before individuals
  • Money: budget reviews that ask whether spending matches stated priorities

The failure process deserves particular attention because how a group treats bad news is culture in concentrate. Edmondson's original finding has since matured into one of the most replicated literatures in organizational psychology, with a recent review confirming psychological safety as a durable predictor of team learning and performance across settings and decades (Edmondson and Bransby 2023). The loop itself is teachable. A mistake gets flagged early and the messenger is thanked for surfacing it. A review happens within the week, the fix is implemented and the story of that sequence gets retold until it becomes the expected pattern. Run that loop consistently and no poster is required.

Move 4Measure behavior, not recall

Most organizations measure values by recall, asking whether staff can recite them, but recall tests marketing rather than behavior. The machine learning work cited earlier makes the same point at scale, because culture only became predictive when researchers measured it from behavior and language rather than from official statements (Li et al. 2021). Better evidence includes:

  • Promotion audits: whether the last five promotions embody the stated values or contradict them
  • Regrettable attrition: who is leaving and what their exits reveal about what is tolerated
  • Review tone: whether debriefs trend toward learning or blame
  • Calendar and budget analysis: whether leader time and money track stated priorities
  • Decision consistency: whether the same standards hold under pressure as in calm
What gets measured becomes the message

Staff read measurement the way players read team selection. Whatever leadership counts, inspects and reports on is understood as what truly matters regardless of what the posters say. Choosing behavioral measures is therefore not an analytics decision but a cultural one.

05Sequencing: the first ninety days

Scope beats scale in culture work, because attempting to operationalize every value at once produces motion without traction. A tighter sequence compounds.

  • Days 1 to 30: choose one value and define its two or three critical behaviors with the people who will live them. Ownership matters here because a group protects what it helped build.
  • Days 31 to 60: rebuild two processes so the behaviors become structural. At least one should involve consequence, such as promotion criteria or resource allocation, because consequence is what separates culture from communication.
  • Days 61 to 90: instrument two behavioral measures and review the evidence with leadership. Close with one question. Is there anything we said that we are not yet doing?

06Stewardship: the posture that closes the gap

Everything above describes mechanism, but mechanism alone does not explain why some leaders sustain this work when it becomes inconvenient. The research literature offers a name for what those leaders have: stewardship. Davis, Schoorman and Donaldson formalized stewardship theory as a counterpoint to the assumption that managers are self interested agents who must be monitored and controlled, proposing instead that people can be intrinsically motivated to act in the long term interests of the collective (Davis, Schoorman and Donaldson 1997). Hernandez's later work defined stewardship as the extent to which an individual willingly places the long term best interests of a group ahead of personal goals, arguing that it grows from a felt sense of psychological ownership over shared outcomes (Hernandez 2012). Peter Block gave the idea its most practical early treatment, defining stewardship as the choice to serve over self interest and arguing that leaders build accountable cultures by distributing ownership and responsibility rather than by hoarding control (Block 1993).

Applied to the values-culture gap the implication is direct. A steward does not treat culture as a communications asset to be managed but as an inheritance held in trust. That reframing changes how every mechanism in this document is handled. Promotions become acts of guardianship because they decide what the next generation learns to imitate. Budgets become statements about what the organization actually protects. Responses to failure become the stories future staff will be told about what this place does under pressure. None of these decisions feel like culture work in the moment, which is exactly why they are.

The concept also runs far older and deeper than the management literature. In te ao Māori the ethic of kaitiakitanga describes guardianship: the responsibility to care for what has been inherited and to pass it on in better condition than it was received. Scholars have shown how this ethic translates into organizational life, where leaders act as custodians who create wellbeing and wealth across generations rather than extracting value within a single tenure (Spiller et al. 2011). It is a standard worth borrowing in any performance environment, because it reframes the central question of leadership. The question stops being what the culture can do for this season's results and becomes what condition the culture will be in when it is handed to whoever comes next.

This is the deepest reason to close the gap between values and culture. It has little to do with efficiency and everything to do with trust. Every time an organization publishes a value and behaves otherwise it teaches people that words are decorative, while every time a stated value shows up in a hard decision it teaches the opposite lesson with equal force. Leaders are stewards of that lesson whether they intend to be or not. Culture is never what you claim. It is what you build, reward and repeat until it becomes what people do when no one is watching.

The steward's question

What condition will the culture be in when it is handed to whoever comes next?
